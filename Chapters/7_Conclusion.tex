\section{CONCLUSION}
This paper examined how ability-diverse teams enact access in video creation workshops with blind and visually impaired learners. Across seven workshops conducted with 10 BVI participants in two UK sites, combining multimodal conversation analysis with reflexive thematic analysis, we asked what barriers shape skill-learning in ability-diverse teams and what collaborative patterns emerge as participants navigate them.

We identified three interlocking asymmetries that structured collaboration: expertise gaps between video and access pedagogies, fragmentation across tool ecosystems, and incompatibilities between visual-spatial and screen-reader interaction paradigms. We documented four collaborative patterns through which participants navigated these asymmetries: direct haptic mediation, cross-modal translation, task-specific delegation, and peer-to-peer assistance. The patterns are not deterministic functions of vision pairings but recurrent strategies organised by the access work each performs, with delegation in particular holding an internal tension between agentic hand-off and pre-emptive sighted-grab.

We proposed dynamic standard-making as a generative reframing of access: the situated co-construction of temporary interactional standards between specific collaborators that enables particular learning moments to proceed. Rather than contesting Universal Design for Learning, dynamic standard-making complements it by accounting for what cannot be designed in advance. Where UDL provides architectural baselines for learner variability, dynamic standard-making is the moment-to-moment work that fills the gaps no baseline can anticipate. The two are coupled: stronger universal-design provision reduces the volume of standard-making required, but some standard-making will always remain because no design can anticipate every collaborator and every task.

The findings carry implications for accessible video tools (exposing workflow operations such as playhead positioning at a granularity screen readers can address; supporting cross-modal translation directly in-application; orienting helpers to gesture grammar before they assist), for inclusive curricula (treating the dyad or triad as the unit of pedagogical attention; teaching dynamic standard-making as an explicit skill; briefing helpers as part of orientation), and for accessibility research more broadly (taking dynamic standard-making as a unit of analysis rather than a residual category). These directions are grounded in the specific patterns we observed under the conditions we describe in our methods, and we read the contribution as a starting point for further work on how access is collectively achieved in ability-diverse settings.
