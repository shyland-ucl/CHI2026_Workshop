\section{Introduction}
Video has become the dominant medium of contemporary digital communication, fundamentally reshaping how people participate in social and cultural life \cite{ha_global_2022}. For blind and visually impaired (BVI) people, video creation is more than entertainment: it is a means of self-representation, advocacy, and community building \cite{emara_its_2025,lyu_because_2024,niu_please_2024}. Studies of BVI vloggers and bloggers report wellbeing gains alongside expressive freedom, peer connection, and visibility \cite{seo_understanding_2021,zhang_understanding_2023}. Yet historically, BVI communities have been under-represented and frequently excluded from mainstream media production due to inaccessible tools, scarce training opportunities, and systemic ableism \cite{lyu_because_2024,raymond_examination_2024,lyu_i_2024-1}. Existing studies of BVI creator communities consistently identify video production as a high-demand but poorly supported area of skill development \cite{zhang_understanding_2023,seo_understanding_2021,lyu_i_2024-1}.

HCI has substantially improved media \textit{consumption} for BVI audiences through audio description, accessible viewing, and non-visual design \cite{aydin_towards_2020,natalie_audio_2024,wang_toward_2021}. On the \textit{production} side, a growing body of work proposes accessible editing through script-based interfaces \cite{huh_avscript_2023} and tile-based canvases \cite{lee_altcanvas_2024}. These prototypes demonstrate feasibility but remain at proof-of-concept stage, leaving learners reliant on mainstream applications (iMovie, CapCut, Videoleap) with uneven accessibility across operating systems and tasks. Self-taught BVI creators pioneer workarounds through online communities \cite{lyu_i_2024-1,seo_understanding_2021}, but the complexity of video production (partially accessible tools, visual verification, platform-specific requirements) creates barriers isolated learning struggles to overcome \cite{xiao_understanding_2025,zhang_understanding_2023}.

This learning gap is amplified by the inherently collaborative nature of video production, which spans scripting, recording, editing, and publishing \cite{huh_avscript_2023,xiao_understanding_2025,zhang_understanding_2023}. Recent HCI scholarship has reframed disability-related collaboration through interdependence \cite{bennett_interdependence_2018} and ability-based design \cite{wobbrock_ability-based_2011}, moving beyond helper--helped dynamics toward distributed contributions \cite{xiao_systematic_2024}. Much of this work, however, draws on professional or workplace contexts; we know less about how ability-diverse collaboration is enacted moment-to-moment in skill-learning settings, where participants are simultaneously novices, tutors, support workers, and co-learners. Universal Design for Learning (UDL) \cite{rose_universal_2001,inc_udl_2024} proposes that providing multiple modes of representation, action, and engagement can accommodate diverse learners, but it presumes that those modalities can be designed in advance. What happens when the available tools admit no such pre-arranged provision, and access must be improvised between specific collaborators in specific moments?

To examine these dynamics, we conducted seven video creation workshops with 10 BVI participants across two UK sites between April and July 2025, in partnership with three regional BVI-sector organisations. The workshops were the practical skill-learning component of a wider community-engagement initiative; this paper focuses on the micro-interactional dynamics that emerged during these sessions as participants learned filming and editing alongside ability-diverse teaching teams. Following a participatory action-research approach and combining multimodal conversation analysis with reflexive thematic analysis, we ask:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1:} What barriers shape video creation skill-learning in ability-diverse teams?
    \item \textbf{RQ2:} What collaborative patterns emerge in these settings, and what do they reveal about how accessibility is enacted in practice?
\end{itemize}

We make three contributions. First, we provide an empirical account of three interlocking asymmetries (expertise, tool ecosystems, and interaction paradigms) that shape ability-diverse video creation learning, with attention to production-specific demands (framing, timeline manipulation, visual verification). Second, we document four collaborative patterns through which participants navigated these asymmetries: haptic mediation, cross-modal translation, task-specific delegation, and peer-to-peer assistance. Third, we propose \textit{dynamic standard-making} as a generative reframing of access: the situated co-construction of temporary interactional standards between specific collaborators, complementing Universal Design for Learning by accounting for what cannot be designed in advance. Together, these contributions inform tool development, curriculum design, and broader theory on how access is collectively achieved.
