\section{Method}
We used a mixed-methods design that combined participant observation, video-based conversation analysis, and reflexive thematic analysis to examine ability-diverse collaboration in accessible video creation workshops.

\subsection{Researcher Positionality and Reflexivity}
\label{sec:positionality}
This study was conducted by a research team whose members occupy different relations to the visual abilities and media practices we study. The first author is a sighted researcher in HCI and accessibility who led workshop facilitation and the analysis presented here, occupying the dual role of facilitator and participant-observer across the seven sessions. A second author is a sighted researcher in accessibility and media studies. Two additional authors are blind researchers: one is an active practitioner in media creation who contributed to teaching and design decisions alongside the analytical work; the other works in media studies and contributed to the conceptual framing of the project.

The combination of sighted and blind researchers, and of HCI, accessibility, and media-studies expertise, shaped both how the workshops were designed and how we read the data they produced. We treat insider participation as a strength of this work that also imposes a reflexive responsibility: observations made during facilitation are not neutral records but partial perspectives shaped by our positions in the room. Three implications follow. First, the first author's role as facilitator inevitably influenced what was modelled, what was corrected, and what was attended to in reflection sessions; we make specific facilitation choices visible in \autoref{sec:design-rationale} and revisit their analytical implications in \autoref{sec:limitations}. Second, the partner organisations had pre-existing relationships with most participants, which supported recruitment and trust but may have shaped what participants felt comfortable saying about the workshops in our presence. Third, the team's commitment to accessibility-centred and participatory research framed barriers as collaborative achievements rather than individual deficits, a framing we hold as productive but not neutral.

\subsection{Design Rationale}
\label{sec:design-rationale}
Several design decisions shape what this study can and cannot show. We make them explicit here so that readers can locate our findings within the conditions that produced them.

\paragraph{Use of mainstream applications.}
The workshops centred on iMovie, CapCut, Videoleap, and built-in iOS/Android camera applications, all of which present substantial screen-reader friction. We adopted these tools deliberately. First, public training must work with the tools learners actually have: research prototypes such as AVscript and AltCanvas \cite{huh_avscript_2023,lee_altcanvas_2024} are not publicly deployed, and specialist equipment is prohibitively expensive. Second, BVI creators in our partner communities asked to learn these applications; teaching with bespoke prototypes would have produced non-transferable skills. Third, the friction is itself part of the phenomenon: how ability-diverse teams negotiate access in the tool ecosystem that exists, rather than one a researcher might design.

\paragraph{Hybrid delivery and site-level pacing.}
Delivery mode was determined by participant preference. The [City 1] cohort attended three in-person sessions and one online; [City 2] was online-only due to travel distance and smaller cohort size. [City 2] consequently compressed the Foundation and Skill-Development phases into a single workshop, a pacing decision made with partners rather than an analytic shortcut. Comparison across modes informs \autoref{sec:results} but is not the central object of analysis.

\paragraph{Helper inclusion in observation, not interview.}
Two participants attended with sighted helpers. Helper contributions appear in our findings (\autoref{sec:results}), but we did not interview helpers formally: this choice respected the informal, often goodwill-based status of these relationships and avoided positioning helpers as research subjects without prior preparation. We documented helper--participant interactions through field notes and consent-based video.

\paragraph{Tutor-assistant-trainee interviews.}
We interviewed two BVI tutor-assistant-trainees (TATs) after the workshops concluded. Each partner nominated a community member with moderate media-creation skills who co-planned and co-delivered training, seeding a peer-leadership pathway. Interviewing TATs rather than the full teaching team reflected this role's distinct positioning at the intersection of community insider, learner, and pedagogical contributor.

\paragraph{Facilitated reflection sessions.}
Each workshop closed with a 20--30-minute structured reflection following prepared prompts (challenges, accessibility experiences, peer interactions, suggestions), with participants setting the depth of their responses. We describe these as facilitated rather than guided: the facilitator structured the session for accessibility (clear turn-taking, announced consent checks, screen-reader-compatible note circulation) without steering toward predetermined conclusions.

\subsection{Data Collection}

\paragraph{Participant Observation.}
The first author served as facilitator and participant-observer (\autoref{sec:positionality}), documenting interactions through detailed field notes taken during and immediately after each 3--4-hour session. Field notes focused on cross-ability collaboration, technology-mediated interactions, and accessibility barriers with the workarounds participants developed.

\paragraph{Video Documentation.}
All workshops were video-recorded with prior written consent. Recordings captured screen activity, wide-angle group views, and close-ups of technical demonstrations, enabling analysis of non-verbal communication, spatial arrangements, and helper mediation.

\paragraph{Post-Workshop Reflections.}
Structured reflections (\autoref{sec:design-rationale}) were audio-recorded and transcribed.

\paragraph{Participant Surveys.}
Participants completed an online survey (n=10) with open-ended questions on challenges, collaboration experiences, accessibility breakdowns, and recommendations. The survey was available in screen-reader-compatible and large-print formats; telephone or Zoom administration was offered for verbal completion.

\paragraph{Semi-Structured Interviews.}
Two BVI tutor-assistant-trainees (TATs) participated in 60--90-minute semi-structured interviews exploring pedagogical adaptations, observed collaboration patterns, the mediating role of technology, and recommendations for future workshops. Interviews occurred one month after workshop completion to allow reflective distance (\autoref{sec:design-rationale}).

\subsection{Data Analysis}

We applied a multi-layered analytical approach suited to our diverse data types.

\paragraph{Video-Conversation Analysis.}
We applied Multimodal Ethnomethodological Conversation Analysis (EMCA) following Due et al. \cite{due_distributed_2021}. The first author manually transcribed selected video clips and used ATLAS.ti \cite{atlasti_scientific_software_development_gmbh_atlasti_2023} to code multimodal interactions, capturing both verbal and embodied elements. This approach helped identify ``collaboration episodes'', bounded sequences in which participants worked together to achieve specific video creation goals. Each episode was coded for (1) initiator (BVI participant or sighted instructor), (2) collaboration type, and (3) interaction modality (verbal, visual, tactile). Within each episode we attended particularly to moments where participants did or did not display positive evidence of mutual understanding, drawing on Clark and Brennan's \cite{clark_grounding_1991} account of grounding alongside Due et al.'s distributed-perception framework.

\paragraph{Thematic Analysis.}
Post-workshop reflections, surveys, and semi-structured interviews were analysed thematically following Braun and Clarke's reflexive six-phase approach \cite{braun_reflecting_2019}. Initial codes were generated inductively from participants' descriptions of barriers, enabling factors, and collaborative experiences. These codes were refined through iterative review, producing broader themes about accessibility challenges, learning strategies, and support needs.

Following initial separate analyses, we conducted integration sessions where findings from different data sources were synthesised. This process involved mapping collaboration patterns identified through video analysis against themes from interviews and reflections, with interdependence theory as an organising frame. Contradictions between data sources were noted explicitly and treated as analytic openings rather than disagreements to be resolved.

\paragraph{Coding Process and Integration.}
All initial coding was conducted by the first author and discussed with the research team during regular meetings to review coded excerpts, surface disagreements, and refine code definitions. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus. EMCA findings and thematic analysis findings were then integrated to produce a comprehensive account: collaboration episodes identified through EMCA were contextualised using participants' reflective accounts, while survey responses corroborated or complicated observed interaction patterns.

\subsection{Ethical Considerations}
This study received institutional ethics approval [PROTOCOL NUMBER PLACEHOLDER]. Given the visual nature of video workshops and the varying vision abilities among participants, we developed a multi-modal consent process including verbal explanation, printed documents, and screen-reader-accessible digital forms. Participants could choose their level of data sharing, with options to exclude video footage while maintaining audio participation.

Data management followed institutional guidelines for sensitive data, with video files stored on encrypted servers and access limited to the research team. All identifying information was removed from transcripts, and participants chose pseudonyms for publication.

\subsection{Limitations of the Study Design}
\label{sec:limitations}
The design choices set out in \autoref{sec:design-rationale} bound the claims this study can support. We name the resulting limits explicitly here, focusing on what the findings cannot show rather than re-stating the choices themselves.

\paragraph{Helper variability.}
Helper variability across pairs (digital competence, prior familiarity with the participant) shaped which collaborative episodes were successful and limits the patterns we can claim. The patterns we report are observed under conditions of variable, unbriefed helper participation rather than controlled facilitation, and the absence of formal helper interviews means we cannot triangulate participant accounts of helper interactions with helpers' own accounts.

\paragraph{Mainstream-tool scope.}
The mainstream-tool design choice sets a specific scope. Tool-ecosystem fragmentation in particular is partly produced by this choice and would likely attenuate if accessible-by-design editing prototypes were widely deployed. We therefore claim the asymmetries as patterns observed in the publicly available tool ecosystem, not as intrinsic features of ability-diverse video collaboration as such.

\paragraph{Sample composition.}
The analytic sample comprises 10 BVI participants across two UK sites, weighted toward older adults (40+, n=6) and women (n=8). This composition reflects partner recruitment dynamics rather than sampling intent, and it skews findings toward the experiences of participants whose media-creation aspirations have been shaped by particular generational and gendered routes into digital practice. Younger BVI creators, who may have substantially different baseline digital fluency, are under-represented. Findings should be read as situated rather than generalisable.

\paragraph{Researcher dual role.}
The first author's dual facilitator-observer role shaped which episodes were observed, which were probed in reflection, and how each was interpreted. We mitigate by presenting interaction transcripts directly so that readers can assess our analytical warrant independently of facilitator interpretation.

\paragraph{Single project, iterative curriculum.}
The findings derive from one project, two UK sites, and an iteratively adjusted curriculum; the seven workshops did not present a uniform intervention. We treat the iteration as a strength of the participatory design but note that it complicates direct replication. Future work in different partner, curricular, and geographic contexts will be needed to test the durability of the asymmetries and patterns we identify.
