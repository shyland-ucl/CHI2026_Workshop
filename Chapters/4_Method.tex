\section{Method}
We used a mixed-methods design that combined participant observation, video-based conversation analysis, and reflexive thematic analysis to examine ability-diverse collaboration in accessible video creation workshops.

\subsection{Researcher Positionality and Reflexivity}
\label{sec:positionality}
This study was conducted by a research team whose members occupy different relations to the visual abilities and media practices we study. The first author is a sighted researcher in HCI and accessibility who led workshop facilitation and the analysis presented here, occupying the dual role of facilitator and participant-observer across the seven sessions. A second author is a sighted researcher in accessibility and media studies. Two additional authors are blind researchers: one is an active practitioner in media creation who contributed to teaching and design decisions alongside the analytical work; the other works in media studies and contributed to the conceptual framing of the project.

The combination of sighted and blind researchers, and of HCI, accessibility, and media-studies expertise, shaped both how the workshops were designed and how we read the data they produced. We treat insider participation as a strength of this work that also imposes a reflexive responsibility: observations made during facilitation are not neutral records but partial perspectives shaped by our positions in the room. Three implications follow. First, the first author's role as facilitator inevitably influenced what was modelled, what was corrected, and what was attended to in reflection sessions; we make specific facilitation choices visible in \autoref{sec:design-rationale} and revisit their analytical implications in \autoref{sec:limitations}. Second, the partner organisations had pre-existing relationships with most participants, which supported recruitment and trust but may have shaped what participants felt comfortable saying about the workshops in our presence. Third, the team's commitment to accessibility-centred and participatory research framed barriers as collaborative achievements rather than individual deficits, a framing we hold as productive but not neutral.

\subsection{Design Rationale}
\label{sec:design-rationale}
Several design decisions shape what this study can and cannot show. We make them explicit here so that readers can locate our findings within the conditions that produced them.

\paragraph{Use of mainstream applications.}
The workshops centred on iMovie, CapCut, Videoleap, and built-in iOS and Android camera applications, all of which present substantial screen-reader friction. We adopted these tools deliberately rather than incidentally. First, public training in BVI media creation must work with the tools learners actually have on their own devices: research prototypes such as AVscript and AltCanvas \cite{huh_avscript_2023,lee_altcanvas_2024} are not publicly deployed, and specialist editing equipment is prohibitively expensive for most participants. Second, BVI creators in our partner communities reported using these applications and asked to learn them; teaching exclusively with bespoke prototypes would have produced skills participants could not transfer to their own creative practice. Third, the friction these tools introduce is itself part of the empirical phenomenon we study: how ability-diverse teams negotiate access in the tool ecosystem that exists, rather than one a researcher might design. We return to the trade-offs of this choice in \autoref{sec:limitations}.

\paragraph{Hybrid delivery and site-level pacing.}
Delivery mode was determined by participant preference, not researcher convenience. The [City 1] cohort attended three in-person sessions and one online session, while the [City 2] cohort received online-only delivery, reflecting longer travel distances and a smaller cohort that made in-person sessions logistically untenable. The [City 2] schedule consequently compressed the Foundation and Skill-Development phases into a single workshop; this was a pacing decision made with partners and revisited iteratively, not an analytic shortcut. Comparison across the two delivery modes informs \autoref{sec:results} but is not the central object of analysis.

\paragraph{Helper inclusion in observation, not interview.}
Two participants attended with sighted helpers, who were paid support workers or family members. Helpers shaped the sessions in ways the analysis takes seriously: they often performed device positioning, menu reading, and informal coaching, and these contributions appear in our collaborative-pattern findings (\autoref{sec:results}). We did not interview helpers formally. This choice respected the informal, often goodwill-based status of these relationships and avoided positioning helpers as research subjects without prior preparation. We instead documented helper--participant interactions through field notes and through video where consent permitted. This decision constrains what we can claim about helpers' own experience, a constraint we name in \autoref{sec:limitations}.

\paragraph{Tutor-assistant-trainee interviews.}
We interviewed two BVI tutor-assistant-trainees (TATs) one month after the workshops concluded. The TAT role was a structural feature of the broader project: each partner nominated a community member with moderate media-creation skills who co-planned and co-delivered training, seeding a peer-leadership pathway beyond the workshop series. Interviewing TATs rather than the full teaching team reflected this role's distinct positioning at the intersection of community insider, learner, and pedagogical contributor. The one-month interval was chosen to allow reflective distance after intensive engagement and to reduce recall bias associated with immediate post-session interviews.

\paragraph{Facilitated reflection sessions.}
Each workshop closed with a 20--30-minute structured reflection led by the first author. The session followed prepared prompts (challenges encountered, accessibility experiences, peer interactions, suggestions for the following session) but participants set the depth and direction of their responses. We describe these as facilitated rather than guided to flag that the facilitator structured the session for accessibility (clear turn-taking, audio recording with announced consent checks, screen-reader-compatible note circulation) without steering toward predetermined conclusions.

\subsection{Data Collection}

Data collection occurred across seven workshops conducted between April and July 2025, using multiple methods to capture the complexity of ability-diverse interactions.

\paragraph{Participant Observation.}
The first author served the dual role of workshop facilitator and participant-observer described in \autoref{sec:positionality}, documenting interactions through detailed field notes taken during and immediately after each 3--4-hour session. Field notes focused on (1) instances of cross-ability collaboration, (2) technology-mediated interactions, and (3) accessibility barriers and the workarounds participants developed in response.

\paragraph{Video Documentation.}
All workshops were video-recorded with prior written participant consent obtained before the first session. Recordings captured multiple perspectives, including screen recordings of digital activities, wide-angle views of group interactions, and close-ups of specific technical demonstrations. This visual data proved essential for analysing non-verbal communication, spatial arrangements, and the role of helpers in mediating access.

\paragraph{Post-Workshop Reflections.}
Each workshop concluded with a 20--30-minute facilitated reflection session in which participants discussed their learning experiences, challenges encountered, and collaborative strategies developed. Sessions followed prepared prompts (see \autoref{sec:design-rationale}) but participants set the depth and direction of their responses. Reflections were audio-recorded and transcribed.

\paragraph{Participant Surveys.}
Following completion of all workshops, participants completed an online survey (n=10) with open-ended questions covering perceived challenges, collaboration experiences, accessibility breakdowns, peer interactions, and recommendations for future training. Full survey items are provided in the supplementary materials. The survey was available in screen-reader-compatible and large-print formats, and participants who preferred verbal completion were offered telephone or Zoom administration.

\paragraph{Semi-Structured Interviews.}
Two BVI tutor-assistant-trainees (TATs) participated in 60--90-minute semi-structured interviews exploring their experiences of the teaching process and of cross-ability collaboration. Interview topics included pedagogical adaptations for mixed-ability groups, observed collaboration patterns, the mediating role of technology, and recommendations for future workshops. Interviews were conducted one month after workshop completion to allow reflective distance (rationale in \autoref{sec:design-rationale}).

\subsection{Data Analysis}

We applied a multi-layered analytical approach suited to our diverse data types.

\paragraph{Video-Conversation Analysis.}
We applied Multimodal Ethnomethodological Conversation Analysis (EMCA) following Due et al. \cite{due_distributed_2021}. The first author manually transcribed selected video clips and used ATLAS.ti \cite{atlasti_scientific_software_development_gmbh_atlasti_2023} to code multimodal interactions, capturing both verbal and embodied elements. This approach helped identify ``collaboration episodes'', bounded sequences in which participants worked together to achieve specific video creation goals. Each episode was coded for (1) initiator (BVI participant or sighted instructor), (2) collaboration type, and (3) interaction modality (verbal, visual, tactile).

\paragraph{Thematic Analysis.}
Post-workshop reflections, surveys, and semi-structured interviews were analysed thematically following Braun and Clarke's reflexive six-phase approach \cite{braun_reflecting_2019}. Initial codes were generated inductively from participants' descriptions of barriers, facilitators, and collaborative experiences. These codes were refined through iterative review, producing broader themes about accessibility challenges, learning strategies, and support needs.

Following initial separate analyses, we conducted integration sessions where findings from different data sources were synthesised. This process involved mapping collaboration patterns identified through video analysis against themes from interviews and reflections, with interdependence theory as an organising frame. Contradictions between data sources were noted explicitly and treated as analytic openings rather than disagreements to be resolved.

\paragraph{Coding Process and Integration.}
All initial coding was conducted by the first author and discussed with the research team during regular meetings to review coded excerpts, surface disagreements, and refine code definitions. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus. EMCA findings and thematic analysis findings were then integrated to produce a comprehensive account: collaboration episodes identified through EMCA were contextualised using participants' reflective accounts, while survey responses corroborated or complicated observed interaction patterns.

\subsection{Ethical Considerations}
This study received institutional ethics approval [PROTOCOL NUMBER PLACEHOLDER]. Given the visual nature of video workshops and the varying vision abilities among participants, we developed a multi-modal consent process including verbal explanation, printed documents, and screen-reader-accessible digital forms. Participants could choose their level of data sharing, with options to exclude video footage while maintaining audio participation.

Data management followed institutional guidelines for sensitive data, with video files stored on encrypted servers and access limited to the research team. All identifying information was removed from transcripts, and participants chose pseudonyms for publication.

\subsection{Limitations of the Study Design}
\label{sec:limitations}
This study is bounded in ways that shape how its findings should be read. We name them explicitly here.

\paragraph{Helper variability.}
As described in \autoref{sec:design-rationale}, two participants attended with sighted helpers whose digital competence and familiarity with the participant varied considerably. Some helper--participant pairs coordinated smoothly; others contributed little beyond minimal prompts. This variation affected what counted as a successful collaborative episode in our data and may obscure patterns that more uniform helper preparation would surface. The collaborative patterns we report are therefore patterns observed under conditions of helper variability rather than under controlled facilitation, and the absence of formal interviews with helpers means we cannot triangulate participant accounts of helper interactions with helpers' own accounts.

\paragraph{Mainstream-tool trade-off.}
The decision to centre publicly deployed applications produces a specific kind of evidence (how teams enact access in the tools that exist) and forecloses another (how teams might collaborate when tools are themselves designed for ability-diverse use). Some of the asymmetries we identify, particularly tool-ecosystem fragmentation, are partially consequences of this choice and would likely attenuate if research prototypes such as AVscript or AltCanvas were available at scale. We treat this as a scope condition rather than a confound, but it should temper any claim that the asymmetries reported are intrinsic to ability-diverse collaboration as such.

\paragraph{Sample composition.}
The analytic sample comprises 10 BVI participants across two UK sites, weighted toward older adults (40+, n=6) and women (n=8). This composition reflects partner recruitment dynamics rather than sampling intent, and it skews findings toward the experiences of participants whose media-creation aspirations have been shaped by particular generational and gendered routes into digital practice. Younger BVI creators, who may have substantially different baseline digital fluency, are under-represented. Findings should be read as situated rather than generalisable.

\paragraph{Researcher dual role.}
The first author's facilitation role inevitably shaped which collaborative episodes were observed, which were probed in reflection, and how each was interpreted. We have foregrounded this in \autoref{sec:positionality} and present findings through direct interaction transcripts wherever possible, so that readers can assess the warrant for our analytical claims independently of facilitator interpretation.

\paragraph{Single project, iterative curriculum.}
The findings derive from one project run with three regional BVI-sector partners across two UK sites. The curriculum was iteratively adjusted across sessions as a feature of participatory action research, which means the seven workshops did not present a uniform intervention. We treat this iteration as a strength of the participatory approach but acknowledge that it complicates direct replication. Future work with different partners, curricula, and geographic contexts will be needed to test the durability of the asymmetries and patterns we identify.
