\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Accessible Media Creation for BVI Creator}
BVI creators use personal media to tell their own stories, educate others, and build community identity. First-person blogging is seen as authentic and helps expand public knowledge, fighting negative stereotypes \cite{emara_its_2025}. On video platforms, creators explain disability norms, advocate for change, teach skills, and show how everyday accommodations are expertise, these benefits extend beyond skill acquisition to include increased confidence, social participation, and sense of agency in digital spaces \cite{seo_understanding_2021}. Beyond individual work, advocacy programs that teach media production connect activists, NGOs, and broadcasters, helping reduce stigma and support long-term participation \cite{brylla_communicative_2024}.

Even with more opportunities, creators still face barriers in capturing, editing, and publishing content. A community study found that while most BVI participants tried making videos, almost all hit problems with editing interfaces, visual feedback, and platform accessibility. Timeline-based interfaces, icons, and preview windows don't work well with screen readers \cite{zhang_understanding_2023}. Huh et al. \cite{huh_avscript_2023} found four main challenges: can't access visual content (like objects and settings), can't check visual quality (lighting, blur), hard to navigate, and poor screen-reader support. These problems often force creators to rely on sighted help or do only basic edits. In places with limited resources, creators work with trusted partners and use AI tools to handle visual tasks while keeping agency of their work \cite{xiao_understanding_2025}.

New systems are changing how editing works by separating editing tasks from visual manipulation. Huh et al. \cite{huh_avscript_2023} replaced the standard timeline with a text-based interface that combines transcripts, scene descriptions, and quality information, making it easier for BVI editors to navigate and select content. Lee et al. \cite{lee_altcanvas_2024} created a system for non-visual design that lets users place objects on a grid they can navigate with accessible feedback.  This gives control over layout and meaning without needing to see the visual canvas. 

While these accessible design prototypes demonstrate technical possibilities, their limited public availability highlights a critical gap between research and practice. This inaccessibility of accessibility tools underscores the need for educational approaches that leverage existing, publicly available tools like Seeing AI and built-in device features, while teaching collaborative strategies that compensate for tool limitations. 

\subsection{Inclusive Education and Collaborative Learning for BVI people}

BVI media education aims not only to “make media accessible,” but to teach production as access practice, building media literacy, authorship, and critical framing skills in a visually dominant curriculum \cite{shaheen_exploring_2024}. Recent work with BLV youth and students documents unmet digital access needs and the centrality of accessible video and course media in higher education, underscoring that instruction must pair content with accessible delivery and assessment \cite{shaheen_exploring_2024,wilkens_using_2025}. These programs argue for explicit media-literacy scaffolds tailored to visual impaired (VI) learners (e.g., narrative codes, shot language, critique rubrics) alongside alternative sensory channels and accessible artefacts. 

Collaborative and inclusive pedagogy is a key theme in this research. Large-scale and regional studies highlight teacher collaboration (teach with and without vision impairment, specialists) as a lever for implementing inclusion when resources and materials are uneven. Co-planning, co-teaching, and shared adaptation of materials are essential \cite{maesala_overcoming_2024,strogilos_meta-synthesis_2023}. Coordination also extends to families and transition services, where cross-actor collaboration predicts better outcomes for VI learners \cite{travers_collaboration_2024}. Reviews show that teacher attitudes, training, and environment shape whether collaboration becomes substantive or nominal, reinforcing the need for institutionally supported co-teaching and professional development pathways \cite{miyauchi_systematic_2020}. 

HCI research provides useful ideas for ability-diverse collaboration. Ability-based design prioritizes adapting systems and tasks to users’ abilities rather than normalizing to sighted templates \cite{nolte_implementing_2022,wobbrock_ability-based_2011}. Interdependence reframes access as collectively produced, foregrounding the legitimate labour of coordination and mutual aid in mixed-ability teams \cite{bennett_interdependence_2018}. Xiao et al.'s \cite{xiao_systematic_2024} review identifies collaboration patterns (ability sharing and ability combining) and specific technology roles in ability-diverse collaboration: ability channel, ability supporter, ability combiner and communication supporter. Studies of disabled professionals’ collaborative ideation further document barriers in whiteboards and canvases (visual salience, structure, communication setup), arguing for practices and tools that externalize structure and negotiate access needs up front \cite{das_that_2024}. 

Concrete pedagogies show how these principles travel to making. Inclusive STEM-and-media programs support mixed groups around accessible artefacts and roles: astronomy and computing curricula with BLV and sighted peers combine non-visual representations, shared inquiry, and teacher training \cite{hochberg_supporting_2024}; Tactile and tangible approaches create shared workspaces for mixed-visual-ability collaboration, for example, gamified tactile navigation for collaborative learning, or tangible programming environments that surface workspace awareness and role symmetry, both emphasizing multi-sensory cues, explicit turn-taking, and aligned metaphors \cite{khalaila_streetscape_2025,rocha_awareness_2025}. Emerging education research explores sensory substitution for visual literacy instruction, suggesting pathways to teach “visual” concepts through structured audio/touch sequences and co-constructed annotations \cite{he_charting_2024}.

At the same time, inclusive media education must address persistent pitfalls: teacher awareness and empathy gaps; inconsistent classroom setups; and the risk that “group work” reproduces dependence unless structure redistributes access labour. Recent work highlights the value of educator sensitization and experiential training, and catalogues environmental adjustments (seating, obstacle-free layouts, verbalization of board work) tied to better learning experiences for VI students \cite{ahmad_najmee_classroom_2025,barbieri_through_2025}. Guidance on audio-supported reading and listening fluency also reinforces that assessment and feedback must reflect non-visual pathways, not merely accommodations tacked onto sighted rubrics \cite{richard_jackson_audio-supported_2021}.
