\section{RESULTS}
\label{sec:results}

Across seven workshops, we observed how participants, tutors, and helpers worked together to translate visual editing tasks into accessible practice. We organise findings around the two research questions: barriers (RQ1) and the collaborative patterns through which participants navigated them (RQ2). We attend throughout to features specific to video production, namely framing for capture, visual verification of recorded content, and timeline-based editing, which generate distinctive demands beyond generic mobile-application interaction.

\subsection{RQ1: Three Asymmetries Shaping Ability-Diverse Video Creation Learning}

We describe the observed barriers as three interlocking asymmetries: expertise gaps between video and access pedagogies, fragmentation across tool ecosystems, and incompatibilities between visual-spatial and screen-reader interaction paradigms. These asymmetries manifested differently in co-located and remote sessions but shaped collaboration consistently across tutor--participant, participant--helper, participant--participant, and tutor--helper relationships. ``Helpers'' here refers to support workers or family members accompanying some participants (\autoref{sec:design-rationale}).

\subsubsection{Expertise asymmetry: video pedagogy versus access pedagogy}

Tutors held strong video production expertise, but translating this knowledge into accessible instruction surfaced fundamental gaps between visual and non-visual pedagogy.

\paragraph{Translation gap between visual and non-visual mental models.}
Tutors with years of timeline editing experience struggled to convey timeline navigation non-visually. P3 ([City 2]): ``Some of the words you're using here, like position the playhead\ldots I've got absolutely no idea about the wording.'' Timeline manipulation is inherently spatial-temporal; translating it into VoiceOver semantics requires reframing the playhead as a position in element-order traversal, not as a draggable cursor. Tutors trained in the visual paradigm often lacked the vocabulary for this reframing.

\paragraph{Tutor--helper coordination gap.}
Helpers took on assistance tasks (device positioning, noting steps, reading menu states) with effectiveness varying by digital competence and prior familiarity. Without role briefing or VoiceOver orientation, helper contributions remained uneven, and tutors absorbed the variability into their pacing.

\paragraph{Documentation and retention gap.}
The absence of accessible, stepwise instructional artefacts shifted learning into short-term memory. P4 ([City 2]): ``I couldn't find any way for writing them down\ldots it became a memory exercise.'' Without persistent materials, participants could not review or practise between sessions, and tutors re-taught procedures in subsequent meetings. The project iteratively developed accessible workbooks with VoiceOver-compatible instructions, which improved between-session practice: ``The workbook made it easier to remember what we did''; ``having the steps written down reduced the stress of forgetting.'' The gap these workbooks filled illustrates what mainstream video tutorials, designed for sighted self-directed learning, fail to provide.

These gaps were navigated through the cross-modal translation and peer-to-peer assistance patterns described below.

\subsubsection{Tool-ecosystem asymmetry: platform fragmentation}

\paragraph{Application-accessibility incompatibility.}
Core editing applications including CapCut and Videoleap were largely non-functional with screen readers, blocking blind participants despite their motivation and effort. iMovie supported VoiceOver more completely but with significant breakdowns at the timeline.

\paragraph{Operating-system divisions.}
The iOS-centric curriculum reduced engagement for Android users. In [City 1], two Android participants could not follow iOS-specific segments; online, requests for Android coverage (for example Open Camera) received limited follow-through. Even within iOS, device variations created confusion: participants noted that iMovie on iPad uses more hierarchical menus than on iPhone.

\paragraph{Workflow-level friction.}
Participants encountered breakdowns when screen-reader navigation and editor workflows misaligned mid-task. In [City 1] Workshop 2, P6 attempted to trim a clip in iMovie; VoiceOver announced ``timeline, adjustable,'' but determining the playhead's exact position required visual feedback VoiceOver did not provide. P6 could enter the timeline but not act precisely within it --- a video-specific failure mode: editing requires sub-second positioning that the screen-reader vocabulary for ``adjustable'' elements was not designed to support.

These breakdowns were compensated through the haptic mediation and task-specific delegation patterns described below.

\subsubsection{Interaction-paradigm asymmetry: competing navigation models}

\paragraph{Dual instruction burden.}
Each editing technique required parallel explanation across paradigms: visual-spatial cues for magnifier users, element-order and audio labels for screen-reader users. Attempting both tracks simultaneously slowed progress. P4 ([City 2]), previewing a four-minute clip online, noted ``it's just whizzing through it a bit'' --- the playback granularity visual scanning supplies was unavailable through the screen reader.

\paragraph{Cognitive load from paradigm switching.}
Translating each step across visual-spatial references and non-visual element traversal demanded continuous remapping by blind participants and by tutors narrating both tracks. In louder in-person rooms, screen-reader users wore earphones to hear VoiceOver, which improved their access but limited their ability to overhear ad-hoc assistance.

\paragraph{Helper--participant navigation disconnect.}
With screen curtain enabled, displays were not visible by design. Even with it disabled, helpers' mental models did not match VoiceOver's gesture grammar, restricting contributions to minimal prompts (``next one,'' ``confirm,'' ``go back'') and compressing what helpers could sustain.

\paragraph{Group-level access conflicts.}
Mixed-ability groups faced competing needs that could not be simultaneously met. Magnifier-optimised instructions confused screen-reader users, while detailed verbal descriptions slowed those with residual vision. One participant named the tension: ``It's always difficult when you've got a mixture of totally blind and partials\ldots trying to adjust to the different needs.'' Two low-vision participants reported that the accessible workbooks felt geared toward blind users, pointing to a need for dual-design materials. Suggestions to split into ``two different groups\ldots one only screen readers, one uses magnifiers'' surfaced participants' own recognition that some access conflicts may not resolve through universal provision.

These mismatches were navigated through the cross-modal translation and haptic mediation patterns described below.

\subsubsection{Delivery-mode trade-offs}

\paragraph{Co-located affordances and constraints.}
In-person sessions enabled tactile and embodied learning: participants could ``touch it and use it,'' and tutors could adjust posture, framing, or grip. These benefits required intensive individualised facilitation that was difficult to scale when access modalities diverged within the room.

\paragraph{Remote affordances and constraints.}
Online sessions supported replay and self-pacing but limited physical demonstration; stabilisation and tripod setup were hard to convey without hands-on contact. Online delivery also removed the spontaneous peer-check: ``It was harder to know if I was doing it right when I couldn't check with the person next to me.'' File transfer was a further barrier, with cloud storage failing unpredictably for some participants.

\subsection{RQ2: Four Collaborative Patterns and the Access Work They Perform}

Participants developed four recurrent collaborative patterns (\autoref{tab:patterns}): direct haptic mediation, cross-modal translation, task-specific delegation, and peer-to-peer assistance. The patterns are not deterministic functions of vision pairings; the same dyad enacted different patterns at different moments depending on the task. We elaborate each below through representative episodes.

\subsubsection{Pattern 1: Direct Haptic Mediation}

When blind participants worked with sighted or partially sighted collaborators, interactions repeatedly bypassed verbal description in favour of direct physical guidance.

\paragraph{Episode 1-1: Physical placement over verbal direction.}
The recording-button sequence from the [City 1] workshop (\autoref{fig:pattern1}) shows how visual-spatial descriptions failed to provide actionable information for a blind participant. A low-vision tutor instructed: ``so right now, you can click on the record option.'' The instruction lacked any spatial reference; the participant's hand froze mid-air, materially demonstrating spatial disorientation. A 2.0-second gap followed, signalling interactional trouble.

A sighted helper sitting beside the participant intervened with ``where that is?'' The tutor's response, ``Oh, that is right at the middle, at the bottom,'' offered coordinates actionable for a sighted person but non-actionable for the participant: ``middle'' and ``bottom'' presuppose a mental model of the screen as a two-dimensional plane with established orientation, a model that depends on visual access. The helper bypassed the verbal description through direct finger placement on the button; VoiceOver confirmed focus, and the participant executed the double-tap independently. Haptic guidance did not replace agency; it provided the missing spatial input. The standard produced --- though never named as such by participants --- was that spatial reference for on-screen controls would be transmitted through fingertip contact rather than verbal coordinate, reproduced in subsequent recording-button and capture-control interactions across the workshop.

\paragraph{Episode 1-2: Systematic tactile mapping.}
For physical equipment introduction, blind--sighted dyads developed structured exploration routines. In the tripod familiarisation sequence (\autoref{fig:pattern1}), the sighted helper positioned the tripod directly in front of the blind participant, took the participant's right hand and placed it on the phone already mounted, and provided the verbal anchor ``You got the phone.'' Exploration then moved methodically downward, from phone to tripod head to vertical pole, with each component verbally labelled as the participant's hand made contact.

Between guided segments, the participant engaged in independent exploration: fingers examining joints, testing pole stability, rotating the adjustment ring. These autonomous investigations demonstrate active learning rather than passive reception. The sequence concluded with the participant independently retracing the path from phone to clamp, confirming the spatial map's construction through haptic channels. Equipment familiarisation in video work is unusually demanding because rigging, framing, and stability interconnect; tactile mapping built an integrated spatial schema. The standard produced --- guided traversal followed by independent retracing --- became the dyad's canonical introduction sequence for new equipment, reused with the ring light and clip microphone in later sessions.

\subsubsection{Pattern 2: Cross-Modal Translation}

When dyads reached the limit of their joint expertise, they recruited a third party, with one collaborator naming the boundary of their knowledge without abandoning their role.

\paragraph{Episode 2-1: Modality relay through a low-vision tutor.}
In the VoiceOver mode-switch sequence (\autoref{fig:pattern2}), a low-vision tutor identified the current state (``picture,'' not ``video'') and the procedural gap: ``it is not doing the video thing. I am confused why.'' She turned to a sighted facilitator, who provided the key as gesture talk: ``you need to do down swipe.'' The tutor named the boundary: ``I do not know how to do it with VoiceOver.'' After a brief mid-air rehearsal with verbal accompaniment (``you can do up swipe and right swipe''), she signalled uptake (``oh I got it'') and only then relayed the instruction haptically, taking the participant's finger and guiding the motion while narrating the VoiceOver outcome. The low-vision collaborator operated as a modality relay: sighted gesture became embodied skill, then tactile coaching aligned with the screen reader. The three-step chain --- gesture description, embodied rehearsal, haptic relay with synchronous narration --- became the triad's standard for subsequent VoiceOver tasks in the session.

\subsubsection{Pattern 3: Task-Specific Delegation}

Low-vision participants managed independence and accuracy across tasks by switching between solo action and targeted hand-off --- a deliberate strategy for preserving agency under variable visual reliability.

\paragraph{Episode 3-1: Delegation as error prevention.}
In the iPad editing episode (\autoref{fig:pattern2}), a low-vision participant confidently recorded a short clip with the device four inches from their face. When asked to ``click this button, at the bottom, this one,'' the device shifted to roughly two inches, indicating visual strain for smaller controls. Fingers hovered near the region, suggesting approximate knowledge but risk of error. The participant said, ``I will let him do that because I will make mistake,'' and handed the device to a sighted helper. Delegation was framed as error prevention rather than inability. The standard produced: the participant, not the helper, sets the criterion for when to delegate --- hand-off conditions on a specific failure mode (small targets, fine trimming, frame-accurate scrubbing) rather than general capability, and the device returns once the failure mode passes.

\paragraph{The sighted-grab risk inside delegation.}
Pattern 3 holds an internal tension. Delegation that preserved agency in this episode could slide into a ``sighted-grab'' dynamic, where helpers pre-empted vision-critical tasks (framing, fine timeline trimming) without participant invitation. This occurred most often under time pressure. Tutors intervened ad-hoc by verbally returning the device or re-anchoring the participant (``what would you like to do next?''). Participants named the limit candidly; several reported they ``wouldn't have managed the final task without help,'' acknowledging that even intentional delegation could not always maintain the boundary between agentic hand-off and dependency during complex production moments. The same channel that enables agentic hand-off can erode agency when not actively cultivated --- pointing to the importance of explicit helper briefing and facilitator vigilance.

\subsubsection{Pattern 4: Peer-to-Peer Assistance}

In remote workshops, participants with advanced technical skills emerged as peer coaches, providing targeted procedural guidance that preserved learner agency.

\paragraph{Episode 4-1: Spatial navigation expertise.}
When participants struggled with camera-mode switching, a blind participant with extensive iPhone experience offered precise spatial guidance: ``You go to the bottom right hand corner\ldots if you wish to change the video, you go slightly further up than the shutter. And that's where you will find the mode button.'' He specified the gesture mechanics (``It's a flick. You do a vertical flick\ldots either up or down'') and corrected a sighted-paradigm misframing: ``when it comes to the iPhone, there's no swiping\ldots you just double-tap on it and it will change.'' Peer experts share detailed mental maps of interface layouts and correct inherited misframings. Unlike the standards produced in Patterns 1--3, which held for the immediate dyadic interaction, the standard here was a shareable rule (iPhone camera mode is changed by double-tap, not swipe; mode button above the shutter at bottom right) that travelled across participants and persisted beyond the session, functioning as a community-of-practice standard rather than a per-interaction improvisation.

\paragraph{Episode 4-2: Peer equipment improvisation in person.}
In-person sessions in [City 1] surfaced a complementary mode of peer-to-peer assistance organised around the physical demands of recording. After tutor demonstrations of tripod and ring-light use, participants exchanged low-cost alternatives --- leaning a phone against household objects for stability, using whatever the environment afforded as a tripod substitute. One participant captured the value: ``a space where we could learn together and not feel alone.'' Unlike Episode 4-1, this mode operated through shared physical presence and easy passing of device or prop, focused on a video-specific concern (camera stability and framing) with no equivalent in audio production. The standard produced: camera stabilisation and framing are problems solved through environmental affordances and peer experimentation, not only through specialist purchase or tutor instruction, and the resulting workarounds belong in the cohort's collective knowledge alongside the formally taught technique.
