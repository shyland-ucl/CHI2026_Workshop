\section{RESULTS}
\label{sec:results}

Across seven workshops, we observed how participants, tutors, and helpers worked together to translate visual editing tasks into accessible practice. We organise findings around the two research questions: barriers (RQ1) and the collaborative patterns through which participants navigated them (RQ2). We attend throughout to features specific to video production, namely framing for capture, visual verification of recorded content, and timeline-based editing, which generate distinctive demands beyond generic mobile-application interaction.

\subsection{RQ1: Three Asymmetries Shaping Ability-Diverse Video Creation Learning}

We describe the observed barriers as three interlocking asymmetries: expertise gaps between video and access pedagogies, fragmentation across tool ecosystems, and incompatibilities between visual-spatial and screen-reader interaction paradigms. These asymmetries manifested differently in co-located and remote sessions but shaped collaboration consistently across tutor--participant, participant--helper, participant--participant, and tutor--helper relationships. ``Helpers'' here refers to support workers or family members accompanying some participants (\autoref{sec:design-rationale}).

\subsubsection{Expertise asymmetry: video pedagogy versus access pedagogy}

Tutors held strong video production expertise, but translating this knowledge into accessible instruction surfaced fundamental gaps between visual and non-visual pedagogy.

\paragraph{Translation gap between visual and non-visual mental models.}
Tutors who had years of timeline editing experience struggled to convey the mechanics of timeline navigation non-visually. P3 ([City 2]) made the gap explicit: ``Some of the words you're using here, like position the playhead\ldots I've got absolutely no idea about the wording.'' Timeline manipulation in video editing is inherently spatial-temporal: the playhead is a moveable point in a horizontally-arranged track. Translating this into VoiceOver semantics requires reframing the playhead as a position in an element-order traversal, not as a draggable visual cursor. Tutors trained in the visual paradigm often lacked the ready vocabulary for this reframing.

\paragraph{Tutor--helper coordination gap.}
Helpers were not part of formal instruction but inevitably took on assistance tasks: device positioning, noting steps, reading out menu states, and stabilising participants' attention across long sessions. Their effectiveness varied by digital competence and prior familiarity with the participant. Some pairs coordinated smoothly; others contributed little beyond minimal prompts. Because helpers received no role briefing or VoiceOver orientation, the quality of their contribution remained uneven, and tutors absorbed the resulting variability into their pacing.

\paragraph{Documentation and retention gap.}
The absence of accessible, stepwise instructional artefacts (text or audio checklists aligned to VoiceOver and magnifier workflows) shifted learning into short-term memory. P4 ([City 2]) observed: ``I couldn't find any way for writing them down\ldots it became a memory exercise.'' This compounded the pacing asymmetry: without persistent materials, participants could not review or practise between sessions, and tutors had to re-teach procedures in subsequent meetings. After the first workshop, the project iteratively developed accessible workbooks with step-by-step VoiceOver-compatible instructions, which improved between-session practice; the gap they filled illustrates what mainstream video tutorials, designed for sighted self-directed learning, fail to provide for non-visual learners.

\subsubsection{Tool-ecosystem asymmetry: platform fragmentation}

\paragraph{Application-accessibility incompatibility.}
Core editing applications including CapCut and Videoleap were largely non-functional with screen readers, blocking blind participants despite their motivation and effort. iMovie supported VoiceOver more completely but with significant breakdowns at the timeline.

\paragraph{Operating-system divisions.}
The iOS-centric curriculum reduced engagement for Android users. In [City 1], two Android participants could not follow iOS-specific segments; online, requests for Android coverage (for example Open Camera) received limited follow-through. Even within iOS, device variations created confusion: participants noted that iMovie on iPad uses more hierarchical menus than on iPhone.

\paragraph{Workflow-level friction.}
Rather than apps being uniformly unusable, participants encountered practical breakdowns when screen-reader navigation and editor workflows misaligned mid-task, leading to abandoned steps and detours. During [City 1] Workshop 2, P6 attempted to trim a video clip in iMovie. VoiceOver reached and announced ``timeline, adjustable,'' but determining the playhead's exact temporal position required visual feedback that VoiceOver did not provide. P6 could enter the timeline but could not act precisely within it. This is a video-specific failure mode: editing requires sub-second positioning that the screen-reader vocabulary for ``adjustable'' elements was not designed to support.

\subsubsection{Interaction-paradigm asymmetry: competing navigation models}

\paragraph{Dual instruction burden.}
Each editing technique required parallel explanation across paradigms. Magnifier users could follow visual-spatial cues, while screen-reader users needed instructions grounded in element order and audio labels. Attempting both tracks simultaneously slowed progress and introduced confusion. Online, this surfaced as focus-control difficulties; reflecting on previewing a four-minute clip, P4 ([City 2]) noted, ``it's just whizzing through it a bit,'' describing how the playback granularity that visual scanning supplies was unavailable through the screen reader.

\paragraph{Cognitive load from paradigm switching.}
Translating each step across visual-spatial references and non-visual element traversal demanded continuous remapping by blind participants and by tutors who had to narrate both tracks. In louder in-person rooms, several screen-reader users wore earphones to hear VoiceOver; this improved their own access but limited their ability to overhear ad-hoc tutor or peer assistance, compounding cognitive effort.

\paragraph{Helper--participant navigation disconnect.}
With screen curtain enabled, displays were not visible by design, eliminating visual guidance routes for helpers. Even with screen curtain disabled, helpers' mental models did not match VoiceOver's gesture grammar, restricting their contributions to minimal prompts such as ``next one,'' ``confirm,'' and ``go back.'' This compressed the helpers' role and constrained the kinds of collaboration they could sustain.

\paragraph{Group-level access conflicts.}
Mixed-ability groups faced competing needs that could not be simultaneously met. Magnifier-optimised instructions confused screen-reader users, while detailed verbal descriptions for blind participants slowed those with residual vision. One participant named the tension directly: ``It's always difficult when you've got a mixture of totally blind and partials\ldots trying to adjust to the different needs.'' Suggestions to split into ``two different groups\ldots one only screen readers, one uses magnifiers'' surfaced participants' own recognition that some access conflicts may not resolve through universal provision.

\subsubsection{Delivery-mode trade-offs}

\paragraph{Co-located affordances and constraints.}
In-person sessions enabled tactile and embodied learning with cameras, rigs, and phones. Participants could ``touch it and use it,'' and tutors could adjust posture, framing, or grip. These benefits required intensive individualised facilitation that was difficult to scale when access modalities diverged within the room.

\paragraph{Remote affordances and constraints.}
Online sessions supported replay and self-pacing but limited physical demonstration. Concepts such as stabilisation or tripod setup were hard to convey without hands-on contact. File transfer was a recurring barrier: cloud storage failed unpredictably for some participants, blocking cross-device sharing at critical moments.

\subsection{RQ2: Four Collaborative Patterns and the Access Work They Perform}

Across these asymmetries, participants developed recurrent collaborative patterns that we identify as four distinct strategies. The patterns are not deterministic functions of vision pairings; the same dyad enacted different patterns at different moments depending on the task and the resources to hand. We label them by the access work each pattern performs: direct haptic mediation, cross-modal translation, task-specific delegation, and peer-to-peer assistance.

\subsubsection{Pattern 1: Direct Haptic Mediation}

When blind participants worked with sighted or partially sighted collaborators, interactions repeatedly bypassed verbal description in favour of direct physical guidance.

\paragraph{Episode 1-1: Physical placement over verbal direction.}
The recording-button sequence from the [City 1] workshop (\autoref{fig:pattern1}) shows how visual-spatial descriptions failed to provide actionable information for a blind participant, leading to immediate haptic intervention. A low-vision tutor instructed: ``so right now, you can click on the record option.'' The instruction lacked any spatial reference that would enable the participant to locate the button; the participant's hand froze mid-air with no movement toward the phone, materially demonstrating spatial disorientation. The 2.0-second silence that followed is what conversation analysts describe as a ``dispreferred response'' marker, signalling trouble in the interaction.

A sighted helper sitting beside the participant intervened with a question that named the missing element: ``where that is?'' The tutor's response, ``Oh, that is right at the middle, at the bottom,'' offered visual-spatial coordinates immediately actionable for a sighted person but non-actionable for the participant. The framework of ``middle'' and ``bottom'' presupposes a mental model of the screen as a two-dimensional plane with established orientation, a model that depends on visual access for its construction. The helper bypassed the verbal description through direct finger placement on the button; VoiceOver then confirmed the focus, and the participant executed the double-tap independently. Haptic guidance did not replace the participant's agency; it provided the missing spatial input the verbal instruction could not.

\paragraph{Episode 1-2: Systematic tactile mapping.}
For physical equipment introduction, blind--sighted dyads developed structured exploration routines. In the tripod familiarisation sequence (\autoref{fig:pattern1}), the sighted helper positioned the tripod directly in front of the blind participant, took the participant's right hand and placed it on the phone already mounted, and provided the verbal anchor ``You got the phone.'' Exploration then moved methodically downward, from phone to tripod head to vertical pole, with each component verbally labelled as the participant's hand made contact.

Between guided segments, the participant engaged in independent exploration: fingers examining the tripod head's joints, hands testing pole stability, and rotating the adjustment ring experimentally. These autonomous investigations, interspersed between guided movements, demonstrate active learning rather than passive reception. The sequence concluded with the participant independently retracing the path from phone to clamp, confirming the spatial map's construction through haptic channels. Equipment familiarisation in video work is unusually demanding because rigging, framing, and stability are all interconnected; tactile mapping addressed this by building an integrated spatial schema rather than a list of parts.

\subsubsection{Pattern 2: Cross-Modal Translation}

When dyads reached the limit of their joint expertise, they recruited a third party. This pattern appeared when one collaborator named the boundary of their current knowledge and invited joint problem-solving without abandoning their role.

\paragraph{Episode 2-1: Modality relay through a low-vision tutor.}
In the VoiceOver mode-switch sequence (\autoref{fig:pattern2}), a low-vision tutor identified the current state (``picture,'' not ``video'') and the procedural gap: ``it is not doing the video thing. I am confused why.'' She turned to a sighted facilitator, who provided the procedural key as gesture talk: ``you need to do down swipe.'' The tutor differentiated concept and implementation: ``I do not know how to do it with VoiceOver.'' After a brief mid-air rehearsal with verbal accompaniment (``you can do up swipe and right swipe''), she signalled uptake (``oh I got it'') and only then relayed the instruction haptically to the blind participant, taking the participant's finger and guiding the on-screen motion while narrating the VoiceOver outcome (``if you swipe down, it will give you the video option''). The low-vision collaborator operated as a modality relay: sighted gestural instruction became an embodied skill, then became tactile coaching aligned with the screen reader.

\subsubsection{Pattern 3: Task-Specific Delegation}

Low-vision participants actively managed independence and accuracy across tasks, switching between solo action and targeted hand-off. We observed this pattern as a deliberate strategy for preserving agency under conditions of variable visual reliability.

\paragraph{Episode 3-1: Delegation as error prevention.}
In the iPad editing episode (\autoref{fig:pattern2}), a low-vision participant confidently recorded a short clip with the device four inches from their face. When asked to ``click this button, at the bottom, this one,'' the device shifted closer, to roughly two inches, indicating increasing visual strain for smaller controls. Fingers hovered near the region, suggesting approximate knowledge but risk of error. The participant then said, ``I will let him do that because I will make mistake,'' and handed the device to a sighted helper, who selected the control. Delegation was framed as error prevention rather than inability, preserving agency while meeting the task requirement.

\paragraph{The sighted-grab risk inside delegation.}
Pattern 3 holds an internal tension. The delegation that preserved agency in this episode could in other moments slide into a sighted-grab dynamic, where helpers or sighted peers pre-emptively took over vision-critical tasks (camera framing, fine timeline trimming) without participant invitation. We observed this most often when time pressure or perceived inefficiency prompted helpers to act faster than the participant. Tutors sometimes intervened by verbally returning the device or by re-anchoring the participant in the task (``what would you like to do next?''), but these interventions were ad-hoc rather than systematic. The same delegation channel that enabled agentic hand-off could also enable agency erosion when not actively cultivated, a finding that points to the importance of explicit role briefing for helpers and of facilitator vigilance during high-stakes production moments.

\subsubsection{Pattern 4: Peer-to-Peer Assistance}

In remote workshops, participants with advanced technical skills emerged as peer coaches, providing targeted procedural guidance that bridged knowledge gaps while preserving learner agency. These peer experts demonstrated deep understanding of both the technology and its accessibility features.

\paragraph{Episode 4-1: Spatial navigation expertise.}
When participants struggled with camera-mode switching, a blind participant with extensive iPhone experience offered precise spatial guidance: ``You go to the bottom right hand corner. And that is where\ldots if you wish to change the video, you go slightly further up than the shutter. And that's where you will find the mode button.'' He then specified the gesture mechanics: ``It's a flick. You do a vertical flick, so you put your finger on it and then you do a vertical flick either up or down.'' He also corrected misconceptions about navigation methods, demonstrating his VoiceOver expertise: ``May I just say, when it comes to the iPhone, there's no swiping. You go to the bottom right hand corner, you'll see, back camera or front. All you do is just double-tap on it and it will change.'' Peer experts develop and share detailed mental maps of interface layouts, translating spatial relationships into actionable instructions and correcting where less experienced peers have inherited a sighted-paradigm misframing of the interaction.
