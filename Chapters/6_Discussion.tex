\section{DISCUSSION}

The asymmetries and collaborative patterns documented in \autoref{sec:results} together suggest that the central theoretical question raised by ability-diverse video creation is not whether multimodal accommodation is possible (it often is), but how access is produced when no pre-arranged accommodation suffices. We argue that the patterns we observed are best understood as instances of dynamic standard-making: the situated co-construction of temporary interactional standards between specific collaborators that enable particular learning moments to proceed. We develop this argument in three steps. We first situate dynamic standard-making against Universal Design for Learning (UDL), the dominant frame for inclusive education. We then re-read each of our four patterns through the dynamic standard-making lens to show what each contributes empirically. Finally, we draw out implications for accessible video tools, inclusive curricula, and the broader study of access.

\subsection{From Universal Design for Learning to Dynamic Standard-Making}

UDL has shaped contemporary inclusive education by mandating multiple modes of representation, action, and engagement \cite{rose_universal_2001,inc_udl_2024}. Its premise is generative: by anticipating learner variability and providing multiple pathways, designers can reduce the labour of after-the-fact accommodation, with empirical evaluations reporting gains in lesson planning and learner engagement \cite{courey_improved_2013,rao_using_2016}. Our findings do not contest these gains. They instead indicate that UDL's planning logic underspecifies what happens when the available modalities cannot meet a particular learner's needs in a particular moment.

The three asymmetries we identified persisted despite multimodal provision. Tutors offered both visual-spatial and verbal instruction; participants had access to magnifiers, screen readers, and tactile equipment; sessions were delivered in both co-located and remote modes. Yet expertise gaps, tool-ecosystem fragmentation, and interaction-paradigm incompatibilities continued to generate moments where the pre-arranged channels did not align. The four collaborative patterns we observed were not implementations of UDL principles but improvisations across them, generated precisely when no pre-arranged channel sufficed. This corresponds with disability studies critiques arguing that universal-design frameworks default to normative assumptions about what counts as reasonable variability \cite{dolmage_academic_2017,hamraie_building_2017}, and with embodied-cognition accounts that treat sensory modalities as constitutive of distinct ways of knowing rather than equivalent channels carrying the same content \cite{varela_embodied_1991,wilson_six_2002}.

We propose dynamic standard-making as a complementary frame: the situated co-construction of temporary interactional standards between specific collaborators that enable particular learning moments to proceed. The concept draws on Suchman's situated-action theory \cite{suchman_human-machine_2006}, which holds that procedures are produced in interaction rather than executed from plans, and on Wobbrock et al.'s ability-based design (ABD) \cite{wobbrock_ability-based_2011}, which holds that systems should adapt to users' specific abilities. Where ABD specifies a relation between a system and an individual user's relatively stable capacities, dynamic standard-making specifies a relation between two or more collaborators producing a temporary standard for a specific task: the unit of analysis shifts from the user to the dyad or triad, and the temporal scale shifts from the user's ability profile to the duration of a particular interaction. When participants engage in haptic mediation or cross-modal translation, they are not adapting to fixed individual abilities but actively constructing what Lave and Wenger describe as communities of practice \cite{lave_situated_1991} with their own internal logics, sustained for the duration of a particular task. The standards so produced are not failures of universal design; they are the work that universal design cannot anticipate.

The move warrants closer differentiation from adjacent frames that have shaped HCI's account of ability-diverse collaboration. \autoref{tab:concepts} sets dynamic standard-making alongside four near neighbours, each of which shares important commitments with our analysis but takes a different object as its unit of analysis. Where interdependence foregrounds the ongoing collaborative relation \cite{bennett_interdependence_2018}, distributed perception foregrounds the joint achievement of perceiving \cite{due_distributed_2021}, and collective access foregrounds political commitment to mutual aid \cite{mia_mingus_access_2017}, dynamic standard-making foregrounds the \textit{standard itself} as a discrete empirical object: nameable by participants or analysts, reusable across moments within the same collaborators, transferable across cohorts (as in Pattern 4), and susceptible to override (as in the sighted-grab tension within Pattern 3). Situated action \cite{suchman_human-machine_2006} gives us the general insight that plans meet improvisation in execution; our contribution names one particular outcome of such improvisation, the co-constructed rule that outlasts the moment that produced it, and the conditions under which it holds or breaks.

Two consequences follow. First, UDL and dynamic standard-making are complementary rather than opposed: the more comprehensive the universal-design provision, the less moment-to-moment standard-making is required, but some standard-making will always remain because no design can anticipate every collaborator and every task. Second, the analytic question changes. Instead of asking whether an interface accommodates a category of users, we can ask under what conditions ability-diverse teams successfully produce workable standards together, and under what conditions they fail.

\subsection{Dynamic Standard-Making Across the Four Patterns}

Each of our four patterns illustrates dynamic standard-making in a different register, and together they suggest that access is achieved through a repertoire of interactional moves rather than through any single architectural provision.

\paragraph{Haptic mediation as embodied standard-making.}
Direct haptic mediation shows dynamic standard-making at its most physical. The helper's finger placement created an embodied standard for ``where the button is'' that held for that participant, that interface, that moment. Such moments accumulate into what Goodwin calls co-operative transformation zones \cite{goodwin_co-operative_2013}, progressively transforming the substrate of prior interactions. The tactile-mapping episode shows this in miniature: the participant's autonomous re-traversal demonstrated that the embodied standard had been incorporated as a workable spatial schema, not merely received.

\paragraph{Cross-modal translation as relay standard-making.}
Cross-modal translation shows standard-making operating across modalities. The low-vision tutor translated sighted gesture into VoiceOver-compatible action and then haptic coaching, constructing a temporary standard that joined three otherwise incompatible modalities. Success depended on the tutor's willingness to name the boundary of her expertise (``I do not know how to do it with VoiceOver'') and convene a triad rather than abandon the task. This aligns with Due et al.'s distributed perception \cite{due_distributed_2021}, where perception is shared across semiotic agents through bodily and technological positioning.

\paragraph{Task-specific delegation as accountable standard-making.}
Task-specific delegation shows standard-making with a built-in tension. Error-prevention delegation preserved agency by treating hand-off as a deliberate, locally agreed standard for who acted on what; the same channel could slide into sighted-grab when speed overrode participant invitation. Sustaining delegation as accountable standard-making required tutors to re-anchor agency in the participant. Dynamic standard-making is not automatically equitable: it requires reflexive attention to who is making the standard and on whose behalf, resonating with disability-justice critiques of paraprofessional support that creates dependency rather than independence.

\paragraph{Peer-to-peer assistance as distributed standard-making.}
Peer-to-peer assistance shows standard-making sustained beyond the dyad. The blind expert's correction of a sighted-paradigm misframing (``there's no swiping'') established a standard not only for one task but for how to think about iPhone interaction. Online sessions' flatter structure, with equal gallery-view presence, reduced hierarchical asymmetries and made distributed expertise more readily activatable, corresponding with Bennett et al.'s interdependence \cite{bennett_interdependence_2018} and Mingus's collective access \cite{mia_mingus_access_2017}, where access is actively cultivated rather than individually granted.

Across the four patterns, dynamic standard-making operates as a method by which ability-diverse teams produce workable interactional infrastructure on the fly. The patterns are not exhaustive, and the conditions under which each succeeds (helper preparation, willingness to name boundaries, time and trust between collaborators) suggest specific levers for both tool design and pedagogy.

\subsection{Implications for Accessible Video Tools, Curricula, and Practice}

The findings carry implications for accessible video tool design, inclusive curriculum design, and the broader study of access in ability-diverse settings. We frame these as design directions grounded in our specific patterns rather than as general prescriptions.

\paragraph{Tools that support standard-making, not only multimodal access.}
Video tools should expose the workflow operations participants struggled to translate (timeline traversal, playhead positioning, frame selection) at a granularity screen readers can address. ``Adjustable element'' is not enough; ``playhead at 0:23.4 of 1:08.0'' with time-step navigation meets video-editing precision demands. Tools should enable cross-modal translation directly: VoiceOver-augmented preview at speech-appropriate scrubbing rates, haptic timeline-boundary feedback, shared cursors co-controlled with audible position announcements. Tools should also support in-application helper preparation: a brief setup mode orienting sighted helpers to VoiceOver gesture grammar. Web-based video editors offer particular promise, providing consistent behaviour across iOS and Android.

\paragraph{Curricula that teach dynamic standard-making as a skill.}
The unit of pedagogical attention should be the dyad or triad, not the individual learner. Workshop orientation should bring participants and helpers into shared training together, establishing common vocabulary for haptic guidance, cross-modal translation, and accountable delegation before any production technique is taught. Dynamic standard-making itself is teachable: how to name the boundary of one's expertise, convene a triad, delegate without surrendering agency, and recognise inherited sighted-paradigm misframings. Assessment should recognise collaborative achievement alongside individual technique. A brief structured briefing for helpers (gesture grammar, screen curtain, accountable delegation) would substantially reduce the variability we documented.

\paragraph{Dynamic standard-making as a frame beyond video.}
Dynamic standard-making is not specific to video creation. It is the work any ability-diverse team performs when pre-arranged provision is insufficient. Naming the work explicitly opens it to study: when do dyads succeed at standard-making, when do they fail, and what conditions sustain the standards once made? Treating dynamic standard-making as a unit of analysis, rather than as a residual category beneath UDL, would let accessibility research engage with the situated achievement of access in domains as varied as collaborative software development \cite{das_that_2024,bandukda_context_2024}, inclusive making \cite{rocha_awareness_2025,khalaila_streetscape_2025}, and mixed-ability scientific work. It also opens dialogue with disability justice frameworks of collective access \cite{mia_mingus_access_2017}, where standard-making is a form of mutual aid, and with situated-action accounts of computing more broadly \cite{suchman_human-machine_2006}, where pre-given plans always meet improvisation in execution.
