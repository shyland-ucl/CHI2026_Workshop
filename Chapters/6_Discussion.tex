\section{DISCUSSION}

The asymmetries and collaborative patterns documented in \autoref{sec:results} together suggest that the central theoretical question raised by ability-diverse video creation is not whether multimodal accommodation is possible (it often is), but how access is produced when no pre-arranged accommodation suffices. We argue that the patterns we observed are best understood as instances of dynamic standard-making: the situated co-construction of temporary interactional standards between specific collaborators that enable particular learning moments to proceed. We develop this argument in three steps. We first situate dynamic standard-making against Universal Design for Learning (UDL), the dominant frame for inclusive education. We then re-read each of our four patterns through the dynamic standard-making lens to show what each contributes empirically. Finally, we draw out implications for accessible video tools, inclusive curricula, and the broader study of access.

\subsection{From Universal Design for Learning to Dynamic Standard-Making}

UDL has shaped contemporary inclusive education by mandating multiple modes of representation, action, and engagement \cite{rose_universal_2001,inc_udl_2024}. Its premise is generative: by anticipating learner variability and providing multiple pathways, designers can reduce the labour of after-the-fact accommodation, with empirical evaluations reporting gains in lesson planning and learner engagement \cite{courey_improved_2013,rao_using_2016}. Our findings do not contest these gains. They instead indicate that UDL's planning logic underspecifies what happens when the available modalities cannot meet a particular learner's needs in a particular moment.

The three asymmetries we identified persisted despite multimodal provision. Tutors offered both visual-spatial and verbal instruction; participants had access to magnifiers, screen readers, and tactile equipment; sessions were delivered in both co-located and remote modes. Yet expertise gaps, tool-ecosystem fragmentation, and interaction-paradigm incompatibilities continued to generate moments where the pre-arranged channels did not align. The four collaborative patterns we observed were not implementations of UDL principles but improvisations across them, generated precisely when no pre-arranged channel sufficed. This corresponds with disability studies critiques arguing that universal-design frameworks default to normative assumptions about what counts as reasonable variability \cite{dolmage_academic_2017,hamraie_building_2017}, and with embodied-cognition accounts that treat sensory modalities as constitutive of distinct ways of knowing rather than equivalent channels carrying the same content \cite{varela_embodied_1991,wilson_six_2002}.

We propose dynamic standard-making as a complementary frame: the situated co-construction of temporary interactional standards between specific collaborators that enable particular learning moments to proceed. The concept draws on Suchman's situated-action theory \cite{suchman_human-machine_2006}, which holds that procedures are produced in interaction rather than executed from plans, and on Wobbrock et al.'s ability-based design \cite{wobbrock_ability-based_2011}, which holds that systems should adapt to users' specific abilities. Dynamic standard-making extends both into collaborative settings: when participants engage in haptic mediation or cross-modal translation, they are not adapting to fixed individual abilities but actively constructing what Lave and Wenger describe as communities of practice \cite{lave_situated_1991} with their own internal logics, sustained for the duration of a particular task. The standards so produced are not failures of universal design; they are the work that universal design cannot anticipate.

Two consequences follow. First, UDL and dynamic standard-making are complementary rather than opposed: the more comprehensive the universal-design provision, the less moment-to-moment standard-making is required, but some standard-making will always remain because no design can anticipate every collaborator and every task. Second, the analytic question changes. Instead of asking whether an interface accommodates a category of users, we can ask under what conditions ability-diverse teams successfully produce workable standards together, and under what conditions they fail.

\subsection{Dynamic Standard-Making Across the Four Patterns}

Each of our four patterns illustrates dynamic standard-making in a different register, and together they suggest that access is achieved through a repertoire of interactional moves rather than through any single architectural provision.

\paragraph{Haptic mediation as embodied standard-making.}
Direct haptic mediation (Pattern 1) shows dynamic standard-making at its most physical. The helper's finger placement on the record button created a momentary embodied standard for ``where the button is'' that could not be transmitted verbally; the standard held only for that participant, that interface, and that moment. Repeated across sessions, such moments accumulate into what Goodwin describes as co-operative transformation zones \cite{goodwin_co-operative_2013}, where participants progressively transform the substrate of prior interactions. The systematic tactile mapping observed in the tripod episode shows this accumulation in miniature: the participant's autonomous re-traversal at the end of the sequence demonstrated that the embodied standard had been incorporated as a workable spatial schema, not merely received.

\paragraph{Cross-modal translation as relay standard-making.}
Pattern 2 shows dynamic standard-making operating across modalities. The low-vision tutor translated sighted gestural instruction into VoiceOver-compatible action and then into haptic coaching, constructing a temporary standard that joined three otherwise incompatible modalities. The relay's success depended on the tutor's willingness to name the boundary of her own expertise (``I do not know how to do it with VoiceOver'') and to convene a triad rather than abandon the task. This pattern aligns with Due et al.'s account of distributed perception \cite{due_distributed_2021}, where perception-related actions are shared across multiple semiotic agents who must position themselves bodily and technologically to enable joint achievement.

\paragraph{Task-specific delegation as accountable standard-making.}
Pattern 3 shows dynamic standard-making with a built-in tension. Delegation as error prevention preserved agency by treating the hand-off as a deliberate, locally agreed standard for who acted on what. But the same channel exposes a brittleness: it can slide into sighted-grab dynamics when speed or perceived efficiency overrides participant invitation. Sustaining delegation as accountable standard-making, rather than as agency erosion, required tutors to verbally re-anchor agency in the participant. This finding underlines that dynamic standard-making is not automatically equitable; it requires reflexive attention to who is making the standard and on whose behalf, a point that resonates with disability justice critiques of paraprofessional support that creates dependency rather than independence.

\paragraph{Peer-to-peer assistance as distributed standard-making.}
Pattern 4 shows dynamic standard-making sustained beyond the dyad. The blind expert participant's correction of a sighted-paradigm misframing (``there's no swiping'') established a standard not only for one task but for how to think about iPhone interaction more generally. The flatter structure of online sessions, where participants occupied equal visual space in gallery view, reduced the hierarchical asymmetries that ordinarily concentrate authority in the sighted lead and made distributed expertise more readily activatable. This corresponds with Bennett et al.'s interdependence framework \cite{bennett_interdependence_2018} and with Mingus's concept of collective access \cite{mia_mingus_access_2017}, in which access is understood as a shared responsibility actively cultivated rather than as an individual accommodation granted.

Across the four patterns, dynamic standard-making operates as a method by which ability-diverse teams produce workable interactional infrastructure on the fly. The patterns are not exhaustive, and the conditions under which each succeeds (helper preparation, willingness to name boundaries, time and trust between collaborators) suggest specific levers for both tool design and pedagogy.

\subsection{Implications for Accessible Video Tools, Curricula, and Practice}

The findings carry implications for accessible video tool design, inclusive curriculum design, and the broader study of access in ability-diverse settings. We frame these as design directions grounded in our specific patterns rather than as general prescriptions.

\paragraph{Tools that support standard-making, not only multimodal access.}
The asymmetries we documented point toward design moves that complement existing accessibility provision rather than replicating it. Video tools should expose the workflow operations participants struggled to translate (timeline traversal, playhead positioning, frame selection) at a granularity that screen readers can address. ``Adjustable element'' is not enough; ``playhead at 0:23.4 of 1:08.0'' with time-step navigation aligned to common edit decisions would meet the precision video editing demands. Tools should also enable cross-modal translation directly through features such as VoiceOver-augmented preview at speech-appropriate scrubbing rates, haptic feedback for timeline boundaries, and shared cursors that two collaborators can co-control with audible position announcements. Finally, tools should support helper preparation in-application: a brief setup mode that orients a sighted helper to VoiceOver gesture grammar before they attempt to assist. Web-based video editors offer particular promise here, providing consistent behaviour across iOS and Android and updating accessibility provision without device-level constraints.

\paragraph{Curricula that teach dynamic standard-making as a skill.}
The collaborative patterns suggest that the unit of pedagogical attention should be the dyad or triad, not the individual learner. Workshop orientation should bring participants and helpers into shared training together, establishing common vocabulary for haptic guidance, cross-modal translation, and accountable delegation before any production technique is taught. Curriculum content should treat dynamic standard-making itself as teachable: how to name the boundary of one's own expertise, how to convene a triad when needed, how to delegate without surrendering agency, and how to recognise when a sighted-paradigm misframing has been inherited and needs correction. Assessment should recognise collaborative achievement as a valid form of media literacy alongside individual technique. The persistent helper-coordination gap we documented is addressable: a brief structured briefing for helpers (gesture grammar, screen curtain, the accountability of delegation) would substantially reduce the variability we observed.

\paragraph{Dynamic standard-making as a frame beyond video.}
Dynamic standard-making is not specific to video creation. It is the work any ability-diverse team performs when pre-arranged provision is insufficient. Naming the work explicitly opens it to study: when do dyads succeed at standard-making, when do they fail, and what conditions sustain the standards once made? Treating dynamic standard-making as a unit of analysis, rather than as a residual category beneath UDL, would let accessibility research engage with the situated achievement of access in domains as varied as collaborative software development \cite{das_that_2024,bandukda_context_2024}, inclusive making \cite{rocha_awareness_2025,khalaila_streetscape_2025}, and mixed-ability scientific work. It also opens dialogue with disability justice frameworks of collective access \cite{mia_mingus_access_2017}, where standard-making is a form of mutual aid, and with situated-action accounts of computing more broadly \cite{suchman_human-machine_2006}, where pre-given plans always meet improvisation in execution.
